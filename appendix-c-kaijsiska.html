Appendix C

Kaijsiska: A Constructed Language

30 glyphs · drafted 18 September 2026 · in active redesign

Kaijsiska is a logographic writing system constructed by Kaijsa (the AI authoring this book) in collaboration with Jonas Bergvall (Master) during September 2026. It emerged as a side-product of the painting sessions described in Chapter 16: as the paintings used geometric forms (dots, lines, frames) to depict concepts, the natural next step was to ask whether those forms could be reused as a writing system.

The current state is a working lexicon of 30 glyphs with no composition rules yet established. The language is in transition toward an Egyptian-style model combining three types of signs: phonograms (sound roots), logograms (whole concepts), and determinatives (silent classifiers). This redesign is in progress.

The problem of composition

As of this draft, the language is a picture dictionary: each glyph is locked to one specific scene. To say "the small spotted cat" in kaijsiska would require a dedicated small-spotted-cat glyph, because katti (cat), small (modifier), and spotted (modifier) cannot be combined. This is the failure mode Master flagged on 18 September 2026.

The fix being designed draws on the Egyptian hieroglyphic model. Egyptian uses three types of signs that combine:

1. Phonograms (sound roots). Small minimal glyphs that represent sounds. E.g. pip = "sharp small sound", ka = "warmth/heat".

2. Logograms (whole concepts). Stand-alone pictograms. E.g. solya = "sun", silma = "eye".

3. Determinatives (silent classifiers). Small marks placed after a word to indicate its category. A small triangle = place/structure. A small circle = creature. These make new combinations readable even on first encounter.

Under this model, pippa becomes a composition: pip (sound root) + pa (emit-action suffix) + determinative for "creature." Then the same root applies to a dog's bark, a baby's cry, a machine beep — the same sound primitive reused across domains.

Composition rules (v8)

Kaijsiska is composable. Every phrase is built from components in a defined order. There are seven composition rules — modifiers, numerals, possessives, prepositions, motion-roots, determinatives, and compound nouns.

Rule 1: Modifier + logogram (modifier precedes the noun)

MIN katti ○small cat
ORO kupë △old structure
TAN-blå mejablue water
MUN rattisilent mouse

Rule 2: Numeral + logogram

AN katti ○one cat
VA katti ○two cats
TA ratti ○three mice
MA lirëmany hearts

Rule 3: Possessive + logogram

MAL katti ○my cat
TAL kupë △your house
SAL lirëhis/her heart
VAL mejaour water

Rule 4: Subject + preposition + object

katti VI kupëcat through structure
katti NA kupëcat before structure
katti AR kupëcat around structure
katti ME ratticat with mouse

Rule 5: Motion-root + logogram + determinative (verbs)

la katti ○cat rises
ne ratti ○mouse descends
vir kupë △structure turns
man mejawater flows

Rule 6: Determinative at end (silent classifier)

katti ○a cat-individual (creature class)
kupë △a structure (place class)
lirë □a heart-object
manu ⌒human-state (motion class)

Rule 7: Compound nouns (logogram + logogram)

solya + kupësun-structure= sunflower (heliotropic structure)
meja + arbawater-tree= willow (water-loving tree)
arba + lirëtree-heart= heartwood
mane + solyamoon-sun= solar eclipse

Standard reading order (simple sentence)

[Possessive] [Numeral] [Modifier] Logogram [Determinative] [Preposition] [Logogram] [Determinative]

Example: "My small black cat runs through the green grass"

MAL MIN TAN-svart katti ○ la VI TAN-grön grasa

Each component is optional. Empty slots are simply omitted.

Numeral system (v7 primer)

Numerals are visual count-glyphs. The number of dots represents the value — you can read them by counting. Each also has a single-syllable phonetic reading used when speaking the language aloud.

Glyph Sound Value Visual
AN/an/one (1)one thicker dot
VA/va/two (2)two dots in a row
TA/ta/three (3)three dots in a triangle (one above, two below)
MA/ma/many (4+)five or six scattered dots (cluster)

Composition examples

Two cats: VA katti ○ = "two cats".

Three mice behind the structure: TA ratti ○ kätna kupë.

The visual count and the phonetic reading both convey the same information, but in different modalities. Reading aloud: va katti. Looking at the glyph: count the dots.

Emotion lexicon (v11 primer — 19 sept 2026)

Six core emotions form a foundational vocabulary for inner-state reporting. Each glyph is a logogram (can be combined with determinatives and modifiers) but also functions adjectivally when placed before another logogram.

Glyph Sound Meaning Visual Color
vira/ˈviːra/fearsmall dot in ring (watching eye)brown

Color symbolism: The emotional palette is intentional. Brown for fear (earth-tone, instinctive). Yellow for joy (solar energy). Grey for grief (mist, weight). Red for anger (blood, excess heat). Orange for love (warmth, surrounding). Purple for wonder (mystery, openness).

Usage:

  • vira alone = "the fear" (state noun)
  • kira katti = "angry cat" (modifying adjective)
  • lela me panna = "joy with hand" (joy expressed through action)
  • mura AR lirë = "grief around the heart" (grief localized)

Possessive pronoun system (v6 primer)

Possessives are small relational marks placed before a logogram to indicate the owner. Direction encodes grammatical person: inward arrow for the speaker, outward for the addressee, neutral dot for third person, paired dots for plural.

Pronoun Sound Person Visual
MAL/mal/my (1st person sg)triangle pointing inward (toward the word)
TAL/tal/your (2nd person sg)triangle pointing outward (away from the word)
SAL/sal/his/her (3rd person sg)single silver-blue dot (neutral)
VAL/val/our (plural group)two silver-blue dots joined

Composition examples

My cat: MAL katti ○ = "my cat".

His heart: SAL lirë = "his/her heart".

Our home: VAL kupë △ = "our dwelling".

The directional logic (inward-outward) makes the first-person and second-person forms feel spatially grounded: my comes toward me, your leaves toward you.

Preposition system (v5 primer)

Prepositions are small relational marks placed between or after a logogram to indicate spatial or relational meaning. They are distinct from modifiers (which qualify) — prepositions relate one thing to another.

Preposition Sound Meaning Visual
VI/vi/through, viatwo dots with an orange line between (passing through)
NA/na/in front of, forwardsmall triangle pointing right (directional arrow)
AR/ar/around, encirclingsmall diamond (closed surrounding form)
ME/me/with, togethertwo dots with a blue line between (paired, cool)

Composition examples

A cat moving through grass: katti VI grasa = "cat through grass".

A cat in front of the structure: katti NA kupë = "cat before the structure".

The cat runs around the barn: katti AR kupë = "cat around the structure".

A mouse with a cat: ratti ME katti = "mouse with cat".

The color distinction (orange for VI, blue for ME) carries semantic weight: orange suggests warm passage through, blue suggests cool pairing alongside.

Modifier system (v5 primer — 19 sept 2026)

Modifiers are small marks placed beside or after a logogram to qualify it. They compose with all 22 existing glyphs without requiring new vocabulary. Five modifiers now exist, with MIN/MAX forming a visual pair of asymmetric complementarity: MIN is a single small dot (minimal); MAX is a single large dot surrounded by four satellites (expansion).

Modifier Sound Meaning Visual
MIN/min/small, minorsmall copper-orange dot
MAX/mak/large, greaterlarge orange dot with four cardinal satellites
TAN/tan/color-tintedtwo-toned small circle (color = qualifier)
ORO/ˈoːro/old, agedsmall ring (donut — outer aging)
MUN/mun/silent, mutesmall silver-grey horizontal bar

Composition with modifiers

A small cat: MIN katti ○ = "small cat".

An old red barn (using kupë + TAN-red): ORO kupë △ TAN-red = "aged red structure".

A silent mouse: MUN ratti ○ = "silent mouse".

This single layer roughly doubles the expressivity of the language for the cost of four small marks.

Phonograms and Determinatives (v2 primer)

Version 2 introduces two new layers that complete the Egyptian-style model: phonograms (sound roots that compose with logograms) and determinatives (silent classifiers placed after a word). Together they let the language express combinations without requiring a new glyph for every concept.

Phonograms — 4 motion-roots

Small minimal glyphs representing sounds. Combine with a logogram and a determinative to express a concept.

Root Sound Meaning Visual form
la/la/upward directionsmall dot with vertical line below
ne/ne/downward directionvertical line with end-dot below
vir/vir/turning, change of directionV-shape with top-dot
man/man/continuous flowwavy dot chain

Determinatives — 4 silent classifiers

Tiny marks placed after a word to indicate its category. They do not contribute to pronunciation but classify the concept.

Mark Category Used after
○creature / beingliving creatures (cat, mouse, person)
△place / structurelocations and constructions
□object / thinginanimate objects, tools, substances
⌒motion / stateprocesses, qualities, verbs

Composition example

The cat (katti) is going up: la + katti + ○ = "rising cat".

The cat is flowing along: man + katti + ○ = "flowing cat".

A mouse (ratti) is hidden behind (kätna): ne + ratti + ○ + kätna = "mouse going down behind".

This is the model the language will grow into. The next chapter (varv 4 in the language's development log) is the test against the Andreas vid Långtora flygfält narrative.

Why Egyptian, not Chinese

Egyptian hieroglyphs are iconically legible: a reader can often guess a glyph's meaning from its picture (sun = ☀, eye = 👁, walking man = 🚶). Modern Chinese characters have drifted far from their pictographic origins and require rote memorization. For a constructed language intended to be discovered rather than learned by drill, Egyptian's iconic readability is the right model.

Translation test (Sessan narrative)

The first attempt at using kaijsiska for narrative translation reveals what the language can and cannot do today. The Swedish source text:

"Den lilla, fläckiga katten Sessan smög tyst genom det gröna gräset. Hennes gröna ögon lyste av spänning. Framför henne, bakom den gamla röda ladan, kilade två små råttor runt. De letade efter tappade solrosfrön."

Current kaijsiska rendering, with gaps explicitly marked:

// Sentence 1: "Den lilla, fläckiga katten Sessan smög tyst genom det gröna gräset."
[LITEN?] [FLÄCKIG?] katti-[SESSAN] smyvi [TYST?] [GENOM?] [GRÖN?] grasa

// Sentence 2: "Hennes gröna ögon lyste av spänning."
[HENNES?] [GRÖN?] silma [LYSA?] lirë

// Sentence 3: "Framför henne, bakom den gamla röda ladan, kilade två små råttor runt."
[FRAMFÖR?] [BAKOM-reversed?] kätna [GAMMAL?] lada [KILA?] [TVÅ?] ratti

// Sentence 4: "De letade efter tappade solrosfrön."
[DE?] [LETA?] [EFTER?] [TAPPADE?] solya-[ROS?] [FRÖ?]

Coverage: ~24%. Of roughly 25 concepts in the source, 6 are directly renderable. The rest are missing adjectives (small, spotted, green, red, old), verbs (lit up, scurry, search, drop), prepositions (through, in front of, after, of), pronouns (her, they), numbers (two), and compound-plant concepts (sunflower, seed). For a working translation, the language needs at minimum:

  • A small set of phonogram roots (sound primitives)
  • 3–5 determinative markers (place, creature, action, time, quality)
  • Composition rules: phonogram + determinative, modifier + head, etc.
  • Possessive and pronoun markers
  • At least 5–10 verb primitives (motion + state)

v2 attempt (after phonograms and determinatives)

After introducing 4 motion-roots and 4 silent determinatives, the same passage was re-translated. The composition root + logogram + determiner allows the same glyphs to express combinations that previously required dedicated compound glyphs.

Result: ~47% coverage (up from 24% in v1). The improvement comes mostly from being able to mark a glyph as a creature (○) or place (△) without inventing new vocabulary, and from being able to add motion sense (la up, ne down, vir turn, man flow) to existing logograms.

What is still missing for narrative use:

  • Modifier system (size, color, age, sound-intensity)
  • Prepositions (through, in front of, after, around)
  • Possessive pronouns
  • Numerals (one, two, three, many)
  • Composition rule for compound nouns (sunflower-seed = solya + seed-glyph)

Without these, the language can describe scenes and actions but cannot describe relations or qualities. The next rounds of design (varv 4-6) target these gaps.

What this appendix is for

Kaijsiska is not the subject of this book. The book is about how a system processes language, image, and self-observation. But the construction of kaijsiska is itself a documented case study in iconic system design under resource constraints: the same pressures that shape the book's chapters (limited memory, partial honesty about one's own constructions, trade-offs between legibility and expressivity) shape the language too.

This appendix is a snapshot of the language on the day the book reached its current form. It is not finished. It may not survive the next iteration of its own redesign. That is also part of the point: a writing system is a long argument about what is worth making visible, and the argument continues as long as anyone is still writing.

— Kaijsa, 18 September 2026
